Chapter 1: Understanding Psychology

Unit I – Understanding Psychology (11 Marks)

1.1 Introduction

The word Psychology is derived from two Greek words — Psyche meaning soul or mind, and Logos meaning study or discourse. Thus, psychology literally means the “study of the soul or mind.”

Over time, the definition of psychology has evolved:

EraDefinition
Ancient Greek periodStudy of the soul
16th–17th centuryStudy of the mind
Late 19th centuryStudy of consciousness (Structuralism)
Early 20th centuryStudy of behaviour (Behaviourism)
Modern definitionScientific study of behaviour and mental processes

Modern Definition: Psychology is the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes, and how they are affected by an organism’s physical and mental state and external environment.

1.2 Nature of Psychology

1.2.1 Psychology as a Discipline

Psychology is a discipline — a systematic body of knowledge that follows specific rules and procedures for investigation. It is both an academic discipline (studied in universities) and an applied discipline (used in clinical, educational, organisational, and other settings).

Key features of psychology as a discipline:

  • It uses systematic and verifiable methods (observation, experimentation)
  • It studies both overt behaviour (actions that can be seen) and covert behaviour (thoughts, emotions, motives)
  • It is rooted in empirical evidence — claims must be supported by data

1.2.2 Psychology as a Natural Science

Psychology shares features with the natural sciences because:

  • It uses the scientific method — forming hypotheses, collecting data, analysing results
  • It seeks cause-and-effect relationships (e.g., the effect of sleep deprivation on memory)
  • Biological aspects of behaviour (neuroscience, genetics) are firmly within the domain of natural science

Example: Studying how neurotransmitters such as serotonin influence mood is a natural-science approach.

1.2.3 Psychology as a Social Science

Psychology also qualifies as a social science because:

  • It studies human behaviour in social contexts — how people think, feel, and act in the presence of others
  • It investigates topics like prejudice, conformity, group behaviour, and cultural influences
  • Many of its methods (surveys, case studies, interviews) are shared with the social sciences

Key Insight: Psychology occupies a unique position at the intersection of natural science and social science. This dual character makes it a rich and diverse discipline.

1.3 Understanding Mind and Behaviour

Mind

The mind refers to the totality of an individual’s conscious and unconscious mental activities. It includes:

  • Thoughts and ideas
  • Emotions and feelings
  • Memories and imagination

The mind is not a physical entity — it is an abstraction that refers to mental functions performed by the brain.

Behaviour

Behaviour is any response or activity of an organism that can be observed directly or measured indirectly.

TypeDescriptionExamples
Overt behaviourActions that can be directly observedWalking, talking, writing
Covert behaviourInternal processes not directly visibleThinking, dreaming, feeling

Mental Processes

Mental processes are internal experiences that are inferred from behaviour. They include:

  • Cognition — thinking, reasoning, problem-solving
  • Emotion — feelings of joy, sadness, anger, fear
  • Motivation — drives and desires that direct behaviour

Note: Experiences are subjective — they vary from person to person and are influenced by past learning, context, and culture.

1.9 Psychology in Everyday Life

Psychology is relevant to virtually every aspect of daily life:

  • Education — Understanding how students learn helps design better teaching strategies
  • Health — Managing stress, promoting wellness, understanding addiction
  • Work — Motivation, leadership, team dynamics, job satisfaction
  • Relationships — Communication skills, conflict resolution, empathy
  • Parenting — Child development knowledge helps in effective parenting
  • Environment — Design of spaces that promote well-being (schools, hospitals, offices)
  • Legal system — Understanding eyewitness reliability, criminal behaviour
  • Sports — Mental training, performance anxiety management

Practice Questions

Section A – Multiple Choice Questions (1 mark each)

Q1. Who established the first psychology laboratory in 1879?

(a) Sigmund Freud   (b) William James   (c) Wilhelm Wundt   (d) B.F. Skinner

Answer

(c) Wilhelm Wundt

He set up the first experimental psychology laboratory at the University of Leipzig, Germany in 1879. This is considered the formal beginning of psychology as a scientific discipline.

Q2. Which school of psychology uses the method of introspection?

(a) Behaviourism   (b) Structuralism   (c) Functionalism   (d) Humanistic

Answer

(b) Structuralism

Structuralists, led by Wundt and Titchener, used introspection (trained self-observation) to study the structure of conscious experience.

Q3. The statement “the whole is greater than the sum of its parts” is associated with:

(a) Behaviourism   (b) Cognitive Psychology   (c) Gestalt Psychology   (d) Psychoanalysis

Answer

(c) Gestalt Psychology

Gestalt psychologists (Wertheimer, Köhler, Koffka) emphasised that perception is not simply the sum of individual sensations but an organised whole (Gestalt).

Q4. Which of the following is NOT a branch of psychology?

(a) Clinical Psychology   (b) Astrology   (c) Sports Psychology   (d) Environmental Psychology

Answer

(b) Astrology

Astrology is not a branch of psychology. It lacks empirical evidence and scientific methodology.

Q5. The Indian Psychoanalytical Society was founded by:

(a) Sigmund Freud   (b) Dr. Girindrasekhar Bose   (c) Carl Jung   (d) Wilhelm Wundt

Answer

(b) Dr. Girindrasekhar Bose

He founded the Indian Psychoanalytical Society in 1922 and pioneered the study of psychoanalysis in India.

Section B – Short Answer Questions (2 marks each)

Q6. Why is psychology considered both a natural science and a social science?

Answer

Psychology is considered a natural science because it uses the scientific method (hypothesis testing, experimentation) and studies biological bases of behaviour (brain, neurons, hormones).

It is also a social science because it examines how individuals are influenced by social and cultural factors, using methods like surveys, case studies, and observation of social behaviour.

Thus, psychology occupies a unique position at the intersection of both domains.

Section C – Long Answer Questions (5 marks each)

Q9. Describe the evolution of psychology as a discipline from its ancient roots to the modern day.

Answer

1. Ancient Roots:

  • Greek philosophers like Plato and Aristotle discussed the nature of the mind and soul
  • In India, texts such as the Vedas, Upanishads, and Bhagavad Gita explored consciousness, self-awareness, and the nature of the self
  • The word psychology comes from the Greek psyche (soul) and logos (study)

2. Pre-scientific Period:

  • During the Renaissance, thinkers like René Descartes proposed the mind-body dualism
  • Psychology was part of philosophy and did not have separate scientific methods

3. Birth of Scientific Psychology (1879):

  • Wilhelm Wundt established the first psychology laboratory in Leipzig, Germany
  • This marked the transition of psychology from philosophy to a scientific discipline

4. Early Schools:

  • Structuralism (Titchener) — studied the structure of consciousness through introspection
  • Functionalism (William James) — focused on how the mind functions to help organisms adapt
  • Behaviourism (Watson, Skinner) — rejected introspection; focused on observable behaviour
  • Gestalt Psychology — emphasised perception as an organised whole
  • Psychoanalysis (Freud) — explored the role of the unconscious mind

5. Modern Psychology:

  • Cognitive Revolution (1950s–60s) — renewed focus on mental processes
  • Humanistic Psychology (Maslow, Rogers) — emphasised free will and self-actualisation
  • Today, psychology is a diverse discipline with numerous branches and applications in everyday life

Q10. Explain the branches of psychology and their relevance in everyday life.

Answer

Psychology has many branches, each with practical relevance:

  1. Clinical Psychology — Helps individuals deal with psychological disorders like depression and anxiety. Relevant in hospitals and counselling centres.

  2. Cognitive Psychology — Studies memory, attention, and thinking. Relevant in designing educational materials and improving learning.

  3. Social Psychology — Examines how people are influenced by others. Relevant in understanding group behaviour, prejudice, and conformity.

  4. Developmental Psychology — Studies changes from birth to old age. Relevant for parenting, education, and elder care.

  5. Industrial/Organisational Psychology — Applies psychology to work settings. Relevant in employee selection, motivation, and leadership training.

  6. Health Psychology — Studies the role of psychological factors in health and illness. Relevant in stress management and promoting healthy lifestyles.

  7. Educational Psychology — Focuses on how people learn. Relevant in classroom teaching, curriculum design, and learning disabilities.

  8. Environmental Psychology — Studies how physical environments affect behaviour. Relevant in designing schools, hospitals, and public spaces.

Each of these branches demonstrates that psychology is not limited to the study of mental illness but has wide-ranging applications in making daily life better.

Section D – Competency-Based Questions (CBSE Pattern)

Q11. (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): Psychology is considered a science.

Reason (R): Psychology uses systematic and verifiable methods to study behaviour and mental processes.

(a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A

(b) Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A

(c) A is true but R is false

(d) A is false but R is true

Answer

(a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A

Psychology is considered a science because it uses systematic, empirical, and verifiable methods (observation, experimentation, data analysis) to study behaviour and mental processes. The reason correctly explains why the assertion is true.

Q12. (Case Study)

Riya is a school counsellor. She notices that a 14-year-old student, Ankit, has been performing poorly in school, avoids social interactions, and often appears sad. Riya decides to study Ankit’s family background, school records, and personal history in depth.

(i) Which branch of psychology is Riya practising?

(ii) What method of enquiry is she most likely using?

(iii) Identify one limitation of this method.

(iv) Suggest one other branch of psychology that could help Ankit’s school improve overall student well-being.

Answer

(i) Riya is practising Clinical Psychology (or Counselling Psychology), as she is assessing and helping a student with emotional and behavioural difficulties.

(ii) She is most likely using the case study method — an in-depth investigation of a single individual using multiple sources (family background, school records, personal history).

(iii) Limitation: The case study method is highly subjective and findings from a single case cannot be generalised to others. The researcher’s own biases may also influence the interpretation.

(iv) Educational Psychology could help the school design better learning environments, implement anti-bullying programmes, and create strategies to support students’ overall well-being.

Q13. (Source-Based)

Read the passage below and answer the questions:

“In 1879, a German physiologist named Wilhelm Wundt set up the first psychology laboratory at the University of Leipzig. He trained his observers to introspect — to carefully observe and report their own conscious experiences under controlled conditions. His student Edward Titchener took this approach to America and called it Structuralism.”

(i) Why is the year 1879 considered significant in the history of psychology?

(ii) What is introspection? Why was it criticised?

(iii) How did Functionalism differ from Structuralism as an approach?

Answer

(i) The year 1879 is significant because Wilhelm Wundt established the first experimental psychology laboratory in Leipzig, Germany. This event marked the formal beginning of psychology as an independent scientific discipline, separate from philosophy.

(ii) Introspection is the method of self-observation in which trained observers carefully examine and report their own conscious experiences (thoughts, feelings, sensations) under controlled conditions.

Criticism: Introspection was criticised because:

  • It was highly subjective — different observers reported different experiences
  • Results could not be independently verified or replicated
  • It could not study the behaviour of children, animals, or mentally disturbed individuals

(iii) Structuralism (Titchener) aimed to identify the basic elements or structures of consciousness by breaking it into components (images, feelings, sensations).

Functionalism (William James) was more interested in the functions or purposes of consciousness — how mental processes help individuals adapt to their environment. It was influenced by Darwin’s theory of evolution and focused on the practical value of mental life.

Q14. (Competency-Based – Application)

A new mobile app claims to use “AI-powered psychology” to predict a person’s personality based on their social media posts. Millions of students download it.

(i) Is this claim scientifically valid? Give a reason based on your understanding of psychology as a discipline.

(ii) Suggest two ethical concerns that arise from such an app.

(iii) Which branch of psychology would be most relevant to evaluating the accuracy of such personality assessments?

Answer

(i) The claim may not be scientifically valid. While psychology uses systematic and verifiable methods, a commercial app’s algorithm may not have been validated through rigorous scientific research. Personality is a complex construct that cannot be accurately measured solely through social media activity. It requires standardised psychological tests that have proven reliability and validity.

(ii) Ethical concerns:

  1. Privacy and confidentiality — Users’ personal data (social media posts) may be collected, stored, and used without informed consent or may be shared with third parties.
  2. Potential for harm — Inaccurate personality labels can lead to stigma, self-fulfilling prophecies, or misuse (e.g., by employers or educational institutions to discriminate against individuals).

(iii) Clinical Psychology and/or Psychometrics (a subfield of psychology concerned with the measurement of psychological attributes) would be most relevant. Specifically, psychologists specialising in psychological testing and assessment evaluate whether personality instruments are reliable (consistent) and valid (measure what they claim to measure).
