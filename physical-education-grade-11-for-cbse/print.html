Grade 11 Physical Education (CBSE 2025-26)

Chapter 1: Changing Trends & Career in Physical Education

1.1 Concept, Aims & Objectives of Physical Education

Concept of Physical Education

Physical education is an integral part of the total education process. It is a field of endeavor that has as its aim the improvement of human performance through the medium of physical activities that have been selected with a view to realizing this outcome. In modern times, physical education is not merely confined to physical exercises or body building, but it is holistic—focusing on the physical, mental, emotional, and social development of an individual.

Aims & Objectives

The central aim of Physical Education is the wholesome development of an individual, making them physically fit, mentally alert, emotionally balanced, and socially well-adjusted.

The main objectives are:

  1. Physical Development: Developing physical fitness components such as strength, speed, endurance, flexibility, and coordinative abilities.
  2. Mental/Cognitive Development: Acquiring knowledge about body systems, rules of games, health, and disease prevention.
  3. Social Development: Developing leadership qualities, cooperation, sportsmanship, and teamwork.
  4. Emotional Development: Controlling emotions like anger, fear, jealousy, and learning how to handle victory and defeat gracefully.
  5. Neuro-Muscular Coordination: Developing a better relationship between the nervous system and the muscular system to perform smooth, coordinated movements.

Note: Physical education prepares an individual not just for sports, but for life. The holistic approach is what separates physical education from simple physical training.

1.2 Development of Physical Education in India – Post Independence

After India gained independence in 1947, significant steps were taken to promote physical education and sports:

  • 1948: Tara Chand Committee was formed to suggest ways to develop physical education.
  • 1950: The Central Advisory Board of Physical Education and Recreation was established.
  • 1953: Rajkumari Amrit Kaur Coaching Scheme was introduced to provide quality coaching to athletes.
  • 1954: All India Council of Sports was established.
  • 1957: Lakshmibai National Institute of Physical Education (LNIPE) was established in Gwalior, offering specialized degrees in physical education.
  • 1961: Netaji Subhas National Institute of Sports (NSNIS) was set up in Patiala to produce elite coaches.
  • 1984: Sports Authority of India (SAI) was established to promote sports and achieve sporting excellence at the national and international levels.

1.3 Changing Trends in Sports

Modern sports has seen massive technological and material advancements. This section highlights the changing trends.

Playing Surface

Traditionally, sports were played on natural grass, clay, or mud. Today, synthetic surfaces have revolutionized the game:

  • Astroturf (Artificial Grass): Widely used in hockey and football. Provides consistent bounce, requires less maintenance, and is unaffected by weather.
  • Synthetic Tracks: Used in athletics for running events (e.g., Polyurethane tracks). They provide better grip, reduce the chances of injury, and enable athletes to achieve faster times.
  • Wooden & Synthetic Floors: Used indoors for basketball, badminton, and volleyball to provide shock absorption.

Wearable Gear

Wearable technology is a game-changer for monitoring athlete performance and health:

  • Smartwatches and Fitness Trackers: Track heart rate, distance covered, calories burned, and sleep patterns.
  • GPS Vests: Worn by football and rugby players to track movement, speed, and positional data on the field.
  • Smart Clothing: Embedded with sensors to measure muscle activity, breathing rate, and biomechanics.

Sports Equipment

Advancements in material science have drastically changed sports equipment:

  • Carbon Fibre: Used to create lighter and stronger tennis racquets, hockey sticks, and bicycles.
  • Aerodynamic Designs: Helmets, swimsuits (like the LZR Racer), and cycling gear are designed to minimize drag and improve speed.
  • Footwear Innovations: Specialized shoes tailored to specific sports, providing better cushioning, energy return, and ankle support.

Technological Advancements

  • VAR (Video Assistant Referee): Used in football to make accurate decisions regarding goals, penalties, and offsides.
  • Hawk-Eye Technology: Used in cricket and tennis to track the trajectory of the ball and assist in umpiring decisions.
  • Goal-Line Technology: Ensuring whether the ball has completely crossed the goal line.

1.4 Career Options in Physical Education

Physical education is no longer restricted to teaching. It has broadened into a multi-disciplinary field offering diverse career paths:

  1. Teaching Careers: Physical Education Teacher (PET) in schools, Lecturer/Professor in colleges and universities.
  2. Coaching Careers: Sports Coach for schools, clubs, state, or national teams.
  3. Health and Fitness Related Careers: Fitness Trainer, Gym Instructor, Yoga Instructor, Dietician/Sports Nutritionist.
  4. Administration Related Careers: Sports Manager, Event Coordinator, Director of Intramural/Extramural Sports, Sports Facility Manager.
  5. Performance Related Careers: Professional Athlete, Sports Official (Referee/Umpire).
  6. Communication / Media Careers: Sports Journalist, Sports Commentator, Sports Photographer, Sports Broadcaster.

1.5 Khelo India Programme and Fit India Programme

Khelo India Programme

Launched in 2018, the Khelo India (Play India) Programme aims to revive the sports culture in India at the grass-root level by building a strong framework for all sports played in the country and establishing India as a great sporting nation.

  • Objectives: Identifying talent at a young age, providing financial assistance (scholarships), upgrading sports infrastructure, and promoting inclusive sports.
  • Key Events: Khelo India Youth Games, Khelo India University Games, and Khelo India Winter Games.

Fit India Movement

Launched on National Sports Day (August 29, 2019) by Prime Minister Narendra Modi, the Fit India Movement aims to encourage people to adopt physical activity and sports in their everyday lives.

  • Objective: To bring about a behavioral change towards a physically active lifestyle, thereby addressing the rising concern of lifestyle diseases like obesity, diabetes, and hypertension.
  • Key Focus: “Fitness ka dose, aadha ghanta roz” (A dose of fitness, half an hour daily).

Chapter 2: Olympic Value Education

2.1 Olympism – Concept and Olympic Values

Concept of Olympism

Olympism is a philosophy of life, exalting and combining in a balanced whole the qualities of body, will, and mind. Blending sport with culture and education, Olympism seeks to create a way of life based on the joy of effort, the educational value of good example, social responsibility, and respect for universal fundamental ethical principles.

The goal of Olympism is to place sport at the service of the harmonious development of humankind, with a view to promoting a peaceful society concerned with the preservation of human dignity.

Olympic Values

The core values of Olympism are:

  1. Excellence: This value is not just about winning or being the best. It means giving one’s best both on the field of play and in life—enjoying the participation, pushing one’s boundaries, and striving for personal improvement.
  2. Friendship: Sport is a tool for building unity and understanding among individuals and people all over the world, overcoming political, economic, gender, racial, or religious differences.
  3. Respect: This includes respect for oneself, for one’s body, for others, for the rules and regulations of sports, and for the environment. Fair play and the fight against doping and unethical behavior are key components.

2.2 Olympic Value Education

Incorporating Olympic Values into education focuses on several key areas:

  • Joy of Effort: Young people explore and develop their physical, mental, and personal abilities through joyful participation in sports.
  • Fair Play: Learning to play by the rules and exhibiting sportsmanship.
  • Respect for Others: Valuing diversity and demonstrating mutual understanding regardless of background.
  • Pursuit of Excellence: Encouraging individuals to set high goals, persevere, and achieve their personal best.
  • Balance Among Body, Will, and Mind: Promoting holistic development rather than just physical prowess.

2.3 Ancient and Modern Olympics

Ancient Olympics

  • Origin: The Ancient Olympic Games began in 776 BCE in Olympia, Greece.
  • Purpose: They were religious festivals held in honor of Zeus, the king of the Greek gods.
  • Participants: Only freeborn Greek males were allowed to participate. Women were barred from competing or even attending as spectators initially.
  • Events: The games started with a single footrace (the stadion) but eventually expanded to include wrestling, boxing, pankration, equestrian events, and the pentathlon.
  • End: The Ancient Games were banned in 393 CE by the Roman Emperor Theodosius I, who considered them a pagan cult.

Modern Olympics

  • Revival: The Modern Olympic Games were revived by Baron Pierre de Coubertin, a French educator.
  • First Games: The first modern Olympic Games were held in Athens, Greece, in 1896.
  • Difference: The modern games are international, secular, and inclusive (with women’s participation growing steadily over the decades).
  • Vision: Coubertin’s vision was to promote international understanding and peace through sports.

2.4 Olympic Symbols, Motto, Flag, Oath, and Anthem

The Olympic Symbol (Rings)

The Olympic symbol consists of five interlocking rings. These rings represent the union of the five continents (Africa, Americas, Asia, Europe, and Oceania) and the meeting of athletes from throughout the world at the Olympic Games. The colors of the rings—Blue, Yellow, Black, Green, and Red—set against a white background, were chosen because every national flag in the world contains at least one of these colors.

The Olympic Motto

The original motto was “Citius, Altius, Fortius” (Faster, Higher, Stronger). In 2021, the motto was updated to “Citius, Altius, Fortius – Communiter” (Faster, Higher, Stronger – Together) to emphasize solidarity during times of global challenge.

The Olympic Flag

The Olympic flag was created under the guidance of Pierre de Coubertin in 1913 and first flown at the 1920 Antwerp Games. It features the five rings on a white background.

The Olympic Oath

Athletes, judges, and coaches take an oath at the opening ceremonies promising to abide by the rules, commit to sport without doping, and uphold the spirit of sportsmanship.

The Olympic Anthem

Composed by Spyridon Samaras with lyrics by Kostis Palamas, the anthem is played while the Olympic flag is raised during the opening ceremony and lowered during the closing ceremony.

2.5 Olympic Movement Structure

The operational structure of the Olympic Movement is composed of three main constituents:

  1. International Olympic Committee (IOC):

    • The supreme authority of the Olympic Movement.
    • Responsible for choosing the host city and overseeing the general organization of the Games.
    • Headquarters: Lausanne, Switzerland.

  2. National Olympic Committees (NOCs):

    • Responsible for developing, promoting, and protecting the Olympic Movement in their respective countries.
    • e.g., The Indian Olympic Association (IOA) is the NOC for India.
    • They handle selecting and sending athletes representing their country to the Games.

  3. International Sports Federations (IFs):

    • International non-governmental organizations recognized by the IOC that administer one or more sports at the global level.
    • e.g., FIFA (Football), FIBA (Basketball), World Athletics (Track & Field).
    • They establish the rules of their respective sports and manage technical aspects during the Olympic Games.

Chapter 3: Yoga

3.1 Meaning and Importance of Yoga

Meaning of Yoga

The word Yoga is derived from the Sanskrit root ‘Yuj’, which means to join, attach, bind, yoke, or to unite. According to Maharishi Patanjali, “Yoga is the control of the modifications of the mind field” (Yogah chitta vritti nirodhah). It is not merely physical exercise but a holistic science of living that unites the individual soul (Jivatma) with the universal spirit (Paramatma).

Importance of Yoga

  • Physical Purity: Yogic kriyas help in internal cleansing of the body, aiding in the smooth functioning of internal organs.
  • Posture and Flexibility: Asanas correct postural deformities, strengthen muscles, and improve joint flexibility.
  • Disease Prevention: Regular practice boosts immunity and reduces the risk of lifestyle diseases like hypertension, obesity, and diabetes.
  • Reduces Mental Tension: Meditation and Pranayama calm the nervous system, reducing stress, anxiety, and depression.
  • Spiritual Development: Yoga enhances moral values and guides individuals toward self-realization and inner peace.

3.2 Introduction to Ashtanga Yoga

Formulated by Maharishi Patanjali in the Yoga Sutras, Ashtanga Yoga consists of eight logical steps or “limbs” (Ashta = eight, Anga = limb) to achieve the ultimate goal of Samadhi.

  1. Yama (Social Codes): Universal ethical disciplines (Ahimsa, Satya, Asteya, Brahmacharya, Aparigraha).
  2. Niyama (Personal Observances): Individual discipline and purification (Saucha, Santosh, Tapa, Svadhyaya, Ishvara Pranidhana).
  3. Asana (Posture): Firm, comfortable, and steady physical postures.
  4. Pranayama (Breath Control): Regulation and extension of the life-force (Prana) through breathing techniques.
  5. Pratyahara (Withdrawal of Senses): Detaching sensory organs from external objects.
  6. Dharana (Concentration): Fixing the mind on a single object or point.
  7. Dhyana (Meditation): Uninterrupted flow of concentration on the object.
  8. Samadhi (Trance/Superconsciousness): The final stage where the meditator merges with the object of meditation.

3.3 Yogic Kriyas (Shat Karma)

Shat Karmas are six purification procedures meant to cleanse the internal organs, balance the three doshas (Vata, Pitta, Kapha), and prepare the body for Pranayama and meditation.

3.4 Pranayama and Its Types

Concept of Pranayama

Pranayama is composed of two words: Prana (vital life force/breath) and Ayama (control/extension). It involves three phases:

  • Puraka: Inhalation
  • Rechaka: Exhalation
  • Kumbhaka: Retention of breath (Antar Kumbhaka = Internal retention; Bahya Kumbhaka = External retention)

Types of Pranayama

  1. Suryabhedana: Inhaling through the right nostril and exhaling through the left. Increases body heat and vital energy.
  2. Ujjayi: ‘Victorious breath’. A slight constriction in the throat creates an oceanic sound. Warms the body and calms the mind.
  3. Sheetali: Cooling breath through a rolled tongue. Reduces body temperature and quenches thirst.
  4. Sheetkari: Cooling breath by inhaling through clenched teeth. Beneficial in summer; cools the system.
  5. Bhastrika: Bellows breath. Rapid, forceful inhalations and exhalations. Increases oxygen levels instantly.
  6. Bhramari: Bee breath. Exhaling with a humming sound. Highly effective in reducing stress, anger, and anxiety.
  7. Anulom Vilom (Nadi Shodhana): Alternate nostril breathing. Balances the right and left hemispheres of the brain.

3.5 Active Lifestyle and Stress Management Through Yoga

A sedentary lifestyle combined with high psychological stress is the root cause of many modern ailments. Yoga plays a critical role in managing stress and promoting activity.

  • Parasympathetic Nervous System Activation: Asanas like Shavasana and Makarasana, along with slow Pranayama, down-regulate the ‘fight or flight’ response.
  • Mindfulness and Focus: By anchoring attention to the breath and body movements, Yoga breaks the cycle of chronic anxiety and negative rumination.
  • Physical Maintenance: Regular practice maintains bone density, keeps joints lubricated, and preserves cardiovascular health, encouraging an overall active lifestyle into old age.

Chapter 4: Physical Education & Sports for CWSN

4.1 Concept of Disability and Disorder

Disability

A disability is any condition of the body or mind (impairment) that makes it more difficult for the person with the condition to do certain activities (activity limitation) and interact with the world around them (participation restrictions). It is a permanent or long-term reduction in a person’s physical or mental ability. Examples include blindness, deafness, amputations, and paralysis.

Disorder

A disorder usually refers to a disruption of the regular or normal functions of the body or mind. It is often an illness that disrupts normal physical or mental functions. A disorder might not always be outwardly visible (like an intellectual or psychiatric disorder) but can severely impact a person’s life. Examples include Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), and eating disorders. If poorly managed, a continuous disorder can lead to a long-term disability.

4.2 Types of Disability, Causes & Nature

1. Intellectual Disability

Intellectual disability involves significant limitations in intellectual functioning (reasoning, learning, problem solving) and in adaptive behavior (everyday social and practical skills).

  • Nature: Occurs before the age of 18. It affects learning ability, communication, and decision making. IQ levels fall significantly below average.
  • Causes:
    • Genetic conditions (e.g., Down syndrome, Fragile X syndrome).
    • Complications during pregnancy (e.g., maternal infections, alcohol consumption).
    • Problems during birth (e.g., oxygen deprivation).
    • Diseases or toxic exposure later in childhood (e.g., meningitis, lead poisoning).

2. Physical Disability

A physical disability limits a person’s physical functioning, mobility, dexterity, or stamina.

  • Nature: Can be congenital (present from birth) or acquired later in life due to accidents or diseases. It includes conditions affecting the musculoskeletal system or nervous system.
  • Causes:
    • Congenital anomalies (e.g., spina bifida, cerebral palsy).
    • Accidents and severe trauma leading to amputations or spinal cord injuries.
    • Diseases like polio, muscular dystrophy, or severe arthritis.

4.3 Disability Etiquette

Always treat people with disabilities with the same respect and dignity you would extend to anyone else. Following proper disability etiquette fosters an inclusive environment.

  • Speak directly: Talk directly to the person with a disability, not their interpreter, nurse, or companion.
  • Respect personal space: Do not touch or lean on a person’s wheelchair. The wheelchair is a part of their personal body space.
  • Ask before helping: Always ask if assistance is needed before jumping in to help. If the offer is declined, do not insist. If accepted, ask how to help.
  • Use “Person-First” language: Say “a person with a disability” instead of “a disabled person,” emphasizing the individual over the condition.
  • Listen attentively: When speaking with someone who has a speech impairment, listen patiently. Do not interrupt or finish their sentences. Ask them to repeat if you didn’t understand.

4.4 Aim and Objectives of Adaptive Physical Education

Adaptive Physical Education (APE) is specially designed physical education meant to meet the unique needs of children with disabilities (CWSN - Children With Special Needs).

Aim

To help students with disabilities achieve physical, mental, emotional, and social growth proportional to their potential through participation in adapted physical activities.

Objectives

  1. Ensure Safe Participation: Providing modified equipment, rules, and environments so CWSN can participate safely.
  2. Develop Motor Skills: Focusing on fundamental motor skills and patterns to improve mobility and coordination.
  3. Enhance Physical Fitness: Designing specific fitness programs to build strength, flexibility, and cardiovascular endurance suitable to their condition.
  4. Promote Socialization: Encouraging interaction between CWSN and typical peers through inclusive games, reducing isolation.
  5. Boost Self-Esteem: Ensuring active participation and providing opportunities for success to build confidence and self-worth.

6.1 Definitions and Importance

Define Test, Measurement, and Evaluation

  • Test: A tool, instrument, procedure, or technique used to obtain data about specific skills, traits, or components of fitness. For example, a stopwatch is an instrument used in the 50m sprint test.
  • Measurement: The process of obtaining a numerical score or quantitative data based on the test. It refers to the score (e.g., scoring 6.5 seconds in a 50m sprint).
  • Evaluation: The process of interpreting the measurement data and making a qualitative judgment or decision based on it. It gives meaning to the measurement (e.g., concluding that a 6.5-second sprint is an ‘Excellent’ rating for a 15-year-old).

Importance in Sports

  • Setting goals and objectives for athletes.
  • Evaluating the effectiveness of a training program.
  • Classifying students/athletes into homogeneous groups.
  • Diagnosing weaknesses and predicting future performance.
  • Motivating athletes to improve their numerical scores.

  2. 600-Yard Run/Walk: Measures cardiovascular endurance.

6.3 Cardiovascular Fitness: Harvard Step Test & Rockport Test

These tests measure aerobic capacity, which is the efficiency of the heart and lungs during prolonged exercise.

Harvard Step Test

Developed by Brouha et al. (1943) at Harvard University, it is a test of aerobic fitness.

  • Equipment: A bench (20 inches high for men, 16 inches for women), stopwatch, metronome.
  • Procedure: The subject steps up and down on the bench at a rate of 30 steps/minute for 5 minutes (or until exhaustion).
  • Pulse Count: After finishing, the subject rests seated. The pulse is counted during three recovery periods:
    • 1 to 1.5 minutes (Pulse 1)
    • 2 to 2.5 minutes (Pulse 2)
    • 3 to 3.5 minutes (Pulse 3)

Fitness Index Calculation Formula (Long Form): \[ \text{Fitness Index} = \frac{\text{Duration of Exercise in Seconds} \times 100}{2 \times (\text{Pulse}_1 + \text{Pulse}_2 + \text{Pulse}_3)} \]

Calculation Formula (Short Form): \[ \text{Fitness Index} = \frac{\text{Duration of Exercise in Seconds} \times 100}{5.5 \times \text{Pulse}_1} \]

Rockport 1-Mile Walk Test

An alternative cardiovascular fitness test, especially suitable for sedentary or elderly individuals who cannot run or do the step test.

  • Procedure: The subject walks 1 mile (1609 meters) as fast as possible on a track.
  • Measurement: Record the time taken to complete 1 mile. Immediately upon finishing, record the heart rate (beats per minute).

( VO_2 ) Max Calculation (using Weight in lbs, Age in years, Time in mins, HR in bpm): \[ VO_2\text{Max} = 132.853 - (0.0769 \times \text{Weight}) - (0.3877 \times \text{Age}) + (6.315 \times \text{Gender}) - (3.2649 \times \text{Time}) - (0.1565 \times \text{HR}) \] (Note: Gender = 1 for Male, 0 for Female)

6.4 Rikli and Jones Senior Citizen Fitness Test

Designed specifically to assess the functional fitness of older adults (aged 60 and above), enabling them to perform daily activities safely.

  1. Chair Stand Test: Number of full stands from a seated position in 30 seconds. (Lower body strength).
  2. Arm Curl Test: Number of bicep curls completed in 30 seconds holding a dumbbell (5 lbs for women, 8 lbs for men). (Upper body strength).
  3. Chair Sit and Reach Test: Sitting on the edge of a chair, extending one leg straight, and reaching toward the toes. Measures flexibility of the lower back and hamstring muscles.
  4. Back Scratch Test: Trying to touch or overlap the middle fingers of both hands behind the back. Measures upper body (shoulder) flexibility.
  5. Eight Foot Up and Go Test: Getting out of a chair, walking 8 feet around a cone, and returning to a seated position as fast as possible. Measures speed, agility, and balance.
  6. Six-Minute Walk Test: Maximum distance walked in 6 minutes along a 50-yard course. Measures aerobic endurance.

6.5 Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)

BMR represents the minimum number of calories your body needs to perform basic, life-sustaining functions while at rest (e.g., breathing, circulation, cell production).

It is calculated using the Mifflin-St. Jeor Equation:

For Men: \[ \text{BMR} = (10 \times \text{weight in kg}) + (6.25 \times \text{height in cm}) - (5 \times \text{age in years}) + 5 \]

For Women: \[ \text{BMR} = (10 \times \text{weight in kg}) + (6.25 \times \text{height in cm}) - (5 \times \text{age in years}) - 161 \]

6.6 Johnson-Metheny Test of Motor Educability

Motor educability is the ability to learn new motor skills easily and quickly. The test battery consists of 4 items performed on a mat:

  1. Front Roll: Forward gymnastic roll.
  2. Back Roll: Backward roll.
  3. Jumping Half-Turn: Jumping in the air, rotating 180 degrees, and landing on the feet.
  4. Jumping Full-Turn: Jumping in the air, rotating 360 degrees, and completing the rotation symmetrically. (Performance is scored out of a maximum qualitative number of points based on form and execution).

Chapter 7: Fundamentals of Anatomy & Physiology in Sports

7.1 Definitions and Importance

Anatomy

Anatomy is the study of the structure, shape, and size of the human body and the relationship of its parts (e.g., bones, muscles, internal organs).

Physiology

Physiology is the study of the functions of the various systems and organs of the human body (i.e., how the body parts work).

Kinesiology

Kinesiology is the scientific study of human movement, performance, and function by applying the sciences of biomechanics, anatomy, and physiology.

Importance in Physical Education

Understanding anatomy and physiology is crucial for:

  • Preventing sports injuries by understanding structural limits.
  • Designing effective training programs based on physiological adaptations.
  • Improving techniques by understanding muscle recruitment.

7.2 Skeletal System and Joints

The adult human skeleton consists of 206 bones. It serves multiple functions: providing a framework, protecting vital organs, acting as levers for movement, and storing minerals.

Classification of Bones

  1. Long Bones: Give structure and act as levers. (e.g., Femur, Humerus).
  2. Short Bones: Provide support with little movement. (e.g., Carpals in the wrist, Tarsals in the ankle).
  3. Flat Bones: Protect internal organs and provide broad surfaces for muscle attachment. (e.g., Sternum, Scapula, Skull).
  4. Irregular Bones: Peculiar shapes for specific functions. (e.g., Vertebrae).
  5. Sesamoid Bones: Embedded in tendons to reduce friction. (e.g., Patella/kneecap).

Types of Joints

A joint is the articulation point between two or more bones.

  • Immovable Joints (Fibrous): Bones fused together. (e.g., Sutures of the skull).
  • Slightly Movable Joints (Cartilaginous): Bones connected by cartilage allowing slight movement. (e.g., Intervertebral discs).
  • Freely Movable Joints (Synovial): Allow wide range of motion. Contain synovial fluid for lubrication.
    • Hinge Joint: Knee, Elbow (movement in one plane).
    • Ball and Socket Joint: Shoulder, Hip (movement in all directions).
    • Pivot Joint: Neck (rotation).

7.3 The Muscular System

Muscles are tissues capable of contraction, thereby producing motion. There are over 600 skeletal muscles in the body.

Properties of Muscles

  1. Excitability: Ability to respond to a nervous stimulus.
  2. Contractility: Ability to shorten and generate force against resistance.
  3. Extensibility: Ability to be stretched beyond their normal resting length.
  4. Elasticity: Ability to return to their original resting length after being stretched.

Functions of Muscles

  • Producing active body movements (walking, lifting).
  • Maintaining posture against gravity.
  • Generating body heat through continuous microscopic contractions.

7.4 Respiratory and Circulatory Systems

During physical exercise, both systems work in tandem to deliver massive amounts of oxygen to the working muscles and remove carbon dioxide.

Respiratory System Function

The primary function is the exchange of gases (Oxygen in, Carbon Dioxide out) between the atmosphere and the blood through the alveoli in the lungs.

Circulatory System Function

Consists of the heart, blood vessels, and blood.

  • The Heart: Acts as a double pump. The right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs. The left side pumps oxygenated blood to the entire body.
  • During exercise, heart rate (beats per minute) and stroke volume (blood pumped per beat) increase drastically to meet oxygen demands.

7.5 Equilibrium – Dynamic & Static

Equilibrium is a state of balance where all forces acting on the body cancel each other out, resulting in zero net force.

  • Static Equilibrium: When the body is completely at rest (e.g., holding a perfectly still handstand).
  • Dynamic Equilibrium: When the body is moving at a constant velocity (e.g., a gymnast executing a cartwheel or a skater smoothly gliding).

Centre of Gravity (CG)

The Centre of Gravity is the imaginary central point around which the entire weight of the body is evenly distributed. Gravity acts vertically downward from this exact point.

  • In Sports: For better stability (e.g., wrestling, defensive stance in basketball), an athlete bends their knees to lower their Centre of Gravity and widens their feet to increase the base of support.
  • The CG shifts depending on body position. When a high jumper arches their back over the bar (Fosbury Flop), their CG actually passes underneath the bar!

Chapter 8: Fundamentals of Kinesiology & Biomechanics in Sports

8.1 Meaning and Importance

Kinesiology

Kinesiology (from Greek kinesis meaning movement, and logia meaning study) is the scientific study of human or non-human body movement. Applications include biomechanics, orthopedics, physical therapy, and sport psychology.

Biomechanics

Biomechanics is the study of the structure and function of biological systems by means of the methods of mechanics. It applies the laws of physics and mechanics to living organisms, particularly comparing the human body to a machine.

Importance in Sports

  1. Improves Technique: Helps athletes optimize movement to achieve peak performance (e.g., finding the perfect release angle for a javelin).
  2. Equipment Design: Aids in developing aerodynamic gear, shock-absorbing shoes, and lighter rackets.
  3. Injury Prevention: Highlights forces (like sheer stress) acting on joints to avoid hazardous techniques.

8.2 Newton’s Laws of Motion & Their Application in Sports

Sir Isaac Newton formulated three fundamental laws of classical mechanics describing the relationship between a body and the forces acting upon it.

1. Law of Inertia (Newton’s First Law)

“An object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by an external net force.”

  • Formula: \[ \Sigma F = 0 \implies \frac{dv}{dt} = 0 \]
  • Application: A football placed on the ground will not move until a player kicks it (applying external force). A sprinter sprinting down the track will continue moving forward after the finish line until they apply braking forces using muscle friction and gravity.

2. Law of Acceleration (Newton’s Second Law)

“The acceleration of an object as produced by a net force is directly proportional to the magnitude of the net force, in the same direction as the net force, and inversely proportional to the mass of the object.”

  • Formula: \[ F = m \times a \implies a = \frac{F}{m} \]
  • Application: In shot put, the heavier the shot (mass), the more force is required to accelerate it. A larger, stronger athlete can apply more force, accelerating the shot faster, making it travel further.

3. Law of Action and Reaction (Newton’s Third Law)

“For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.”

  • Formula: \[ F_{AB} = -F_{BA} \]
  • Application: When a swimmer pushes the water backward with their hands (action), the water pushes the swimmer forward with an equal force (reaction). When a high jumper pushes down hard onto the ground (action), the ground exerts an enormous upward force (reaction) launching them into the air.

8.3 Types of Levers and Their Application in Sports

A lever is a rigid rod that turns around a fixed point called a Fulcrum (F). The force applied is the Effort (E), and the weight moved is the Load/Resistance (R).

  • Class I Lever (( F ) in the middle): Load - Fulcrum - Effort.

    • Example in Body: Nodding the head. The fulcrum is the top vertebra, load is the front of the skull, effort is the neck muscles pulling down the back of the skull.
    • Sports Example: Rowing an oar.

  • Class II Lever (( R ) in the middle): Fulcrum - Load - Effort.

    • Example in Body: Standing on tiptoes. The fulcrum is the toes, load is body weight on the ball of the foot, effort is the calf muscle pulling the heel up.
    • Sports Example: Doing a push-up.

  • Class III Lever (( E ) in the middle): Fulcrum - Effort - Load.

    • Example in Body: Bicep curl. The fulcrum is the elbow joint, effort is the bicep muscle attached just below the elbow, load is the dumbbell in the hand.
    • Note: Class III levers are the most common in the human body. They favor a wide range of motion and speed over heavy force.

Mechanical Advantage Formula:

\[ MA = \frac{\text{Effort Arm length}}{\text{Resistance Arm length}} \] If ( MA > 1 ), it requires less effort to move a large load (Class II). If ( MA < 1 ), it requires greater effort but moves the load faster and further (Class III).

8.4 Axis and Planes

All human movement takes place in specific geometric planes and around specific geometric axes.

Planes of the Body

  1. Sagittal Plane: Divides the body vertically into left and right halves.
    • Example Movements: Flexion and extension (e.g., running, bicep curls, nodding yes).
  2. Frontal (Coronal) Plane: Divides the body vertically into front and back halves.
    • Example Movements: Abduction and adduction (e.g., jumping jacks, cartwheels).
  3. Transverse (Horizontal) Plane: Divides the body horizontally into top and bottom halves.
    • Example Movements: Rotation (e.g., spinning in ice skating, twisting the torso).

Axes of the Body

  1. Frontal-Horizontal Axis: Passes horizontally from side to side. (Perpendicular to Sagittal plane).
  2. Sagittal-Horizontal Axis: Passes horizontally from front to back. (Perpendicular to Frontal plane).
  3. Vertical (Longitudinal) Axis: Passes straight down from the top of the head. (Perpendicular to Transverse plane).

8.5 Friction and Projectile in Sports

Friction in Sports

Friction is the resistance that one surface or object encounters when moving over another.

  • Static Friction: The force keeping an object stationary (e.g., spikes of track shoes gripping the starting blocks).
  • Dynamic/Kinetic Friction: The opposing force between moving surfaces (e.g., ski blades gliding on snow).
  • Role in Sports: Friction can be advantageous (like basketball shoes squeaking to stop quickly) or disadvantageous (air friction slowing down a cyclist, hence the need for aerodynamic helmets).

Projectile Motion in Sports

An object thrown into space is a projectile. Its path is called its trajectory.

  • Goal in Sports: To achieve maximum horizontal range (e.g., javelin throw, long jump) or vertical height (e.g., high jump).
  • Factors affecting trajectory:
    1. Angle of Release: Theoretically, an angle of 45 degrees achieves the maximum horizontal distance.
    2. Speed/Velocity of Release: Higher speed leads to geater distance.
    3. Height of Release: Launching from a higher point extends the horizontal range.
    4. Air Resistance: Slows down the projectile.

Equation of Trajectory (Range): \[ R = \frac{v^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g} \] (Where R is Range, v is initial velocity, (\theta) is the angle of release, and g is gravity).

Adolescence is a period of “storm and stress” due to massive hormonal and psychological shifts.

  1. Aggressive Behavior: Hormonal fluctuations can cause sudden bursts of anger, often leading to conflict with parents or teachers.
  2. Substance Abuse: Peer pressure and the desire to “fit in” or escape stress can lead to the use of drugs, alcohol, or tobacco.

9.4 Team Cohesion and Sports

Team Cohesion is defined as a dynamic process that reflects the tendency for a group to stick together and remain united in the pursuit of its instrumental objectives and/or for the satisfaction of member affirmative needs.

Types of Cohesion

  1. Task Cohesion: The degree to which team members work together to achieve a specific and identifiable goal (e.g., winning the championship).
  2. Social Cohesion: The degree to which team members like each other and enjoy each other’s company outside of the sport.

Factors Enhancing Team Cohesion (Carron’s Model)

  • Environmental Factors: Size of the team, geographical proximity, contractual obligations.
  • Personal Factors: Individual member’s motivation, background, and dedication to the team.
  • Leadership Factors: The coach’s style (democratic vs. autocratic) and the relationship between coach and players.
  • Team Factors: Past success/failure, team stability, shared experiences.

9.5 Psychological Attributes: Attention, Resilience, Mental Toughness

1. Attention

Attention in sports (often called focus or concentration) is the ability to direct the senses and thought processes to the most relevant cues in the environment while ignoring irrelevant distractions (like a hostile crowd).

  • Example: A batsman entirely focused on the bowler’s release point, ignoring the fielders’ chatter.

2. Resilience

Resilience is the psychological capacity an athlete possesses to quickly recover, rebound, and successfully adapt in the face of adversity, trauma, tragedy, or significant sources of stress (such as a severe injury or a devastating loss).

3. Mental Toughness

Mental toughness is a collection of attributes that allow a person to persevere through difficult circumstances. World-class athletes possess:

  • An unshakable self-belief in their ability to achieve their competition goals.
  • An insatiable desire and internalized drive to succeed.
  • The ability to regain psychological control following unexpected, uncontrollable events.

Chapter 10: Training & Doping in Sports

10.1 Concept and Principles of Sports Training

Sports Training is a pedagogical process based on scientific principles aiming at preparing athletes for higher performances in sports competitions.

Key Principles of Sports Training

  1. Principle of Continuity: Training must be a continuous, year-round process. Long breaks cause a decline in adaptations.
  2. Principle of Overload: For adaptation to occur and fitness to improve, the training load must exceed the body’s normal threshold.
  3. Principle of Specificity: The body adapts specifically to the type of demand placed on it (e.g., a marathon runner must train by running long distances, not lifting maximum weights).
  4. Principle of Individual Differences: Every athlete responds differently to identical training loads due to genetics, age, and background.
  5. Principle of Rest and Recovery: Adaptation physically occurs during the rest period following the overload. Without recovery, overtraining and injury occur.

10.2 Training Load: Overload, Adaptation, and Recovery

The relationship between load, fatigue, and recovery defines the Supercompensation Cycle.

  • Training Load: The physiological and psychological demand placed on an organism during exercise.
  • Overload: Applying a stimulus greater than what the body is accustomed to. This immediately induces fatigue and a temporary drop in performance.
  • Recovery: The period of rest and nutrition following the overload.
  • Adaptation (Supercompensation): Because the body was stressed, it repairs itself during recovery to be stronger/fitter than its baseline level to handle the stress better next time.

10.3 Warming-Up & Limbering Down

Warming-Up

A preparation period consisting of light physical activity prior to a strenuous workout.

  • Types:
    • General Warm-up: Jogging, stretching, calisthenics (raises core temperature).
    • Specific Warm-up: Rehearsing the actual movements of the sport (e.g., a bowler rolling down a few deliveries in cricket).
  • Importance: Increases blood flow to muscles, reduces blood viscosity, lubricates joints, and exponentially decreases the risk of muscle tears.

Limbering Down (Cooling Down)

A period of light exercise and static stretching immediately following an intense workout.

  • Importance: Slowly returns the heart rate to normal, helps flush out lactic acid from the muscles (reducing delayed onset muscle soreness), and prevents blood pooling in the extremities.

10.4 Concept of Skill, Technique, Tactics & Strategies

Understanding the hierarchy of athletic performance:

  1. Skill: An automatized motor action capable of solving a specific movement task effectively. It is the overall ability an athlete acquires (e.g., passing a football).
  2. Technique: The single, scientifically sound, biomechanical method of executing a physical movement (e.g., striking the football with the inside of the foot while locking the ankle).
  3. Tactics: Specific, short-term plans implemented during the game to overcome a specific defensive or offensive setup of the opponent (e.g., exploiting a slow defender on the right wing).
  4. Strategy: The overarching, long-term master plan decided before the competition begins based on a deep analysis of one’s own team and the opponent.

10.5 Concept of Doping and its Disadvantages

What is Doping?

The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) defines doping as the occurrence of one or more anti-doping rule violations, primarily the use of artificial, banned substances or methods (like blood transfusions) to illegally enhance athletic performance.

Common Banned Substances

  • Anabolic Steroids: Used to build muscle mass and strength quickly (causes liver damage, severe aggression/mood swings).
  • Stimulants: Used to increase alertness and reduce fatigue (causes heart attacks, insomnia).
  • Diuretics: Used primarily to mask the presence of other drugs or for rapid weight loss in combat sports (causes severe dehydration, kidney failure).
  • Peptide Hormones (EPO): Increases red blood cell count to boost aerobic capacity (causes blood thickening, strokes).

Disadvantages of Doping

  1. Health Risks: Most banned substances carry extreme, often lethal, side effects (cardiac arrest, liver cancer, irreversible hormonal changes).
  2. Unethical: Completely destroys the core Olympic value of fair play and honest competition.
  3. Career Destruction: Athletes caught doping face public humiliation, stripping of past medals, and multi-year or lifetime bans from their sport.
